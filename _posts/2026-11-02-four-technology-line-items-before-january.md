You'll set next year's budget in the next six weeks. Technology tends to get whatever money remains after payroll, rent and insurance, and then something breaks in March and you pay for it at emergency rates.

Put a number beside these four items before you close the spreadsheet. Each one comes with a way to size it.

## 1. Computers past their support date

Microsoft ended support for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025. Its end-of-support page says the paid Extended Security Updates program keeps security patches flowing to Windows 10 PCs until October 12, 2027 ([Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/end-of-support)). A Windows 10 machine outside that program gets no security updates at all.

Walk the office with a notepad. Record every PC's operating system and age, and mark the ones that can't run Windows 11. Those machines either need an upgrade path, an ESU enrollment with a replacement date, or a spot in next year's hardware budget.

**How to size it:** get a written quote for replacement machines, then multiply by the count on your notepad. Spread the replacements across quarters if the total hurts.

## 2. A network you can see and manage

If your office runs on consumer routers or a mesh kit from a big-box store, budget for managed equipment. Consumer gear shows you little about what sits on your network or why it slowed down, and it leaves you guessing when a device misbehaves.

In 2018 I installed a Ubiquiti UniFi network for a client: a Dream Machine as the gateway, three managed PoE switches and two business-grade access points. Managed PoE switches power the access points through the network cable, and one console covers routing, switching and wireless. That setup lets someone answer "what is on our network" in minutes.

**How to size it:** count your wired drops and the rooms where Wi-Fi drops out, then get a quote for managed switching and business access points sized to that count. Ask the installer to include labeling and a network diagram. The diagram costs an hour of the installer's time and saves the next technician a day of tracing cables.

## 3. An AI policy and one funded pilot

Your staff use AI tools today, approved or not. A written policy costs little and tells them which tools they may use and which data may go into each one. Spell out categories staff can recognize, such as client files, personnel records and financial statements, instead of abstract labels like "sensitive data." Pair it with a small, fixed budget for a single pilot that has a named owner and a success measure you set in advance.

Fund one pilot. Three pilots with no owners turn into three subscriptions you forget to cancel.

**How to size it:** set the pilot budget at the cost of the tool for six months, plus the staff hours to test it. Write the success measure before you spend a dollar.

## 4. An outside look before you commit

You lock in a year of assumptions when you approve a budget. Before you approve yours, have someone with no stake in your vendor relationships read it. Independent IT assessments for firms of 10 to 50 employees run $2,500 to $7,500 ([unio.digital](https://unio.digital/blog/conducting-an-it-infrastructure-assessment-a-checklist)). My own AI Readiness Assessment is $2,500 and the full Technology and AI Assessment is $6,500, and both fees come off the first month if you continue into a retainer.

**How to size it:** compare the fee to the largest technology contract you plan to sign next year. If the contract costs ten times the review, you recover the fee the first time the reviewer catches a bad clause.

## Before you close the spreadsheet

Go down the four items and write a dollar figure or a zero beside each one. A zero is a fine answer as long as you chose it on purpose. If money runs short, fund the unsupported computers first: a machine with no security updates stays exposed every day it stays on the network, and you can push the other three items back a quarter with less risk.
