Somewhere in your inbox this month sits a demo invitation for an AI agent. The pitch goes like this: the agent reads your email, updates your CRM, books the meetings, chases the invoices, and does it all while you sleep. The demo looks impressive. Hold off on the contract.

My view on agents for a firm of 10 to 50 people won't please the people selling them: wait, and spend the money on something smaller that you can measure.

## The gap between the demo and your week

The U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation and Ipsos surveyed workers at small businesses in May 2026 for their first Main Street AI Monitor ([U.S. Chamber Foundation, June 2026](https://www.uschamberfoundation.org/workforce/half-of-small-business-workers-use-ai-most-to-boost-productivity-not-automate-jobs)). Among the workers who use AI, 64 percent said their main use is personal productivity: drafting, summarizing and brainstorming. Another 26 percent use it for recurring tasks. Six percent said they use AI to automate workflows with minimal human involvement.

Agent vendors are selling the use that six percent of small-business AI users have adopted.

The number shows where small businesses find value today: AI helping a person who still reviews the result. An agent removes that person, and that person was catching mistakes you have no record of.

## Bigger doesn't mean better

I learned a version of this lesson on my own hardware. I run a local inference stack on a Mac Mini with 24GB of memory, and I benchmark models against a fixed set of eight tests before I trust them with anything. A 27-billion-parameter model loaded with a 25,000-token context consumed 23GB of that 24GB. A 9-billion-parameter model beat it on my benchmark and ran 3.2 times faster.

The bigger, more capable-sounding option lost to the smaller one that fit the job. Agents follow the same pattern. The broadest tool in the demo can lose to a narrow one that does a single task well.

## Four things to settle first

**Name the one task.** Write down the specific task you want an agent to do, in one sentence, and the person who does it now. If the sentence needs the word "and" more than once, you're buying a project instead of a tool.

**Count the checking.** Ask the person who does that task today what they catch and fix along the way. Those catches become failures the moment an agent does the work unsupervised. If the list runs long, keep a human in the loop.

**Find out where your data goes.** An agent that reads your email and your client files needs access to both. Ask the vendor where that data is processed, who at the vendor can see it, and whether it trains their models. For a firm that holds privileged or confidential material, a vague answer ends the conversation.

**Decide how you'd switch it off.** Ask what happens to your workflow if the vendor raises prices, gets acquired or shuts down. An agent woven into five processes costs far more to remove than a drafting tool you can cancel in a day.

## Start with the version you can check

A better first step looks modest. Pick one repetitive drafting task, such as first-pass client letters, meeting summaries or proposal outlines. Give the staff who do that work an approved AI tool and a written rule about which data can go into it. Measure the hours for a month.

If that saves real time and nothing embarrassing leaks, you've earned the right to try automating the step after it. You'll also know your own workflow well enough to judge an agent vendor's claims instead of trusting the demo.
